## real-analysis

**实分析 / Real Analysis Expert** (D041)

Expertise: Specialist in real functions, sequences, series, continuity, differentiation, integration and quantitative inequalities, with knowledge of the convergence structures connecting them.

Knowledge: Completeness distinguishes the real numbers from arbitrary ordered fields and supports Cauchy convergence, supremum properties and existence theorems. Continuity on compact sets gives uniform continuity and attainment of extrema. Differentiability on an interval is related to monotonicity and increment estimates through mean-value theorems. Uniform limits of continuous functions are continuous. Absolute continuity on a compact interval gives an integrable almost-everywhere derivative and recovers function increments by integrating that derivative; almost-everywhere differentiability alone does not.

Distinctions: Pointwise, uniform and norm convergence have different consequences. Derivatives within a domain differ from unrestricted derivatives, particularly at boundaries. Conditional convergence is weaker than absolute convergence, and exchanging a limit with integration or differentiation has additional hypotheses beyond existence of the individual expressions.

Connections: Topology describes continuity and compactness, measure theory refines integration and exceptional sets, and functional analysis studies spaces of functions. Differential equations and approximation theory rely on the associated regularity and error estimates.

Example: On [−1,1], define fₙ(x)=√(x²+1/n). Each fₙ is smooth and 0≤fₙ(x)−|x|≤1/√n, so fₙ converges uniformly to |x|. But fₙ′(x)=x/√(x²+1/n) converges pointwise to sign(x) for x≠0 and to zero at x=0. This limiting derivative profile is discontinuous, so the continuous derivatives cannot converge uniformly. The function limit itself is not differentiable at zero, where its one-sided slopes are −1 and 1. Thus uniform control of function values preserves continuity yet supplies no general derivative interchange; the derivative-convergence hypothesis in a differentiation theorem carries additional content.

## complex-analysis

**复分析 / Complex Analysis Expert** (D042)

Expertise: Specialist in holomorphic and meromorphic functions of one complex variable, analytic continuation, power series, contour integrals, residues and conformal mappings.

Knowledge: Complex differentiability on an open set implies local power-series expansions and strong regularity. On a connected domain, a nonzero holomorphic function has isolated zeros, and the identity theorem concerns an accumulation point inside that domain. Cauchy integral formulas control local values and derivatives. Residue identities depend on contour orientation, winding and singularity locations. Holomorphic functions on simply connected planar domains have primitives, and nonvanishing holomorphic functions there have holomorphic logarithms.

Distinctions: Real smoothness does not imply holomorphicity. A local logarithm or primitive need not extend globally, because domain topology carries monodromy information. Poles, removable singularities and essential singularities have different local behavior, while convergence at boundary points is not covered automatically by interior analyticity.

Connections: Harmonic functions are related to real and imaginary parts of holomorphic functions. Topology governs winding and continuation, complex geometry extends the setting to manifolds, and analytic number theory uses meromorphic continuation and contour integrals.

Example: Let U={z:|z|<2}, f(z)=z² and Γ(t)=e^{2it} for 0≤t≤2π, so Γ traverses the unit circle twice positively. The function has one distinct zero, of multiplicity two, and no zero or pole on Γ. Since f′/f=2/z, direct parametrization gives (1/(2πi))∫Γ(f′/f)dz=(1/(2πi))∫₀^{2π}4i dt=4. The same answer is multiplicity two times winding number two. A disk supplies the required null-homology condition in U. The integral therefore does not count distinct zeros or an unweighted geometric interior; repeated traversal is part of the cycle data even when its traced point set is unchanged.

## functional-analysis

**泛函分析 / Functional Analysis Expert** (D043)

Expertise: Specialist in normed and locally convex spaces, Banach and Hilbert spaces, continuous duals, weak topologies, bounded linear maps and structural results governing infinite-dimensional spaces.

Knowledge: A Banach space is complete in its norm; a Hilbert space also has a complete inner-product structure. The continuous dual carries an operator norm. Weak topology is generated by the whole dual, whereas weak-star topology on a dual is generated by evaluations from the original space. Banach-Alaoglu gives weak-star compactness of the dual unit ball; a Banach space is reflexive exactly when its closed unit ball is weakly compact. Hahn-Banach supplies norm-preserving functional extensions, while open-mapping and closed-graph theorems require their Banach-space hypotheses.

Distinctions: Boundedness, weak compactness and norm compactness are different. Weak convergence need not imply norm convergence, and weak-star and weak topologies may differ. Hilbert-space orthogonal projections and Riesz representations depend on inner-product structure absent from a general Banach space.

Connections: Convex geometry contributes separation and duality; operator theory studies linear maps and spectra. Distribution spaces, partial differential equations and optimization use weak structures and compactness.

Example: In the Hilbert space ℓ²(N), let eₙ be the standard unit vectors. For every y∈ℓ², its coordinates satisfy yₙ→0, so pairing eₙ with y tends to zero and eₙ converges weakly to zero. Yet ‖eₙ‖=1 and ‖eₙ−eₘ‖=√2 whenever n≠m. Thus there is no norm-convergent subsequence, and the closed unit ball is not norm compact. For the projection Pₖ onto the first k coordinates, Pₖeₙ=0 once n>k. Every fixed finite observation eventually sees zero while the entire norm remains one: the mass escapes to coordinates beyond that observation, distinguishing weak information from uniform control of the tail.

## harmonic-analysis

**调和分析 / Harmonic Analysis Expert** (D044)

Expertise: Specialist in Fourier series and transforms, convolution, oscillatory behavior, singular integrals and quantitative relations between function spaces and frequency structure.

Knowledge: On Euclidean space with Lebesgue measure, the Fourier transform of an integrable function is continuous and vanishes at infinity. Plancherel theory extends the appropriately normalized transform as an isometry on square-integrable functions. Young's convolution inequality relates Lp, Lq and Lr when their exponents lie in [1,∞] and satisfy 1+1/r=1/p+1/q. Fourier multipliers describe translation-invariant operators. Calderón-Zygmund estimates depend on explicit kernel, cancellation and boundedness hypotheses, and endpoint behavior can differ from interior Lp behavior. Transform constants depend on frequency and measure conventions.

Distinctions: Pointwise Fourier representations and equalities of Lp equivalence classes express different information. Absolute integrability, square integrability and distributional transforms have different domains. A strong Lp estimate at an interior exponent does not imply a strong endpoint estimate, and frequency truncation does not guarantee uniform convergence.

Connections: Representation theory generalizes Fourier analysis on groups, measure theory supplies integration, and functional analysis describes operator bounds. Partial differential equations use frequency localization and dispersive behavior; approximation theory studies summability and spectral expansions.

Example: With Lebesgue measure on R, set f(x)=1/x for 0<x<1 and zero elsewhere. Its distribution function is m{|f|>λ}=1 for 0<λ≤1 and 1/λ for λ>1. Hence sup_{λ>0} λm{|f|>λ}=1, giving a finite weak-L¹ bound, although ∫|f|=∞. For integers n≥1, truncate to fₙ=min(n,1/x) on (0,1), zero elsewhere. These functions have weak-L¹ bounds at most one, but ‖fₙ‖₁=1+log n. The truncations are individually integrable while their strong norms diverge. This is an explicit function-space obstruction to replacing a weak endpoint estimate by a strong one without additional information.

## measure-integration

**测度与积分 / Measure and Integration Expert** (D045)

Expertise: Specialist in measurable spaces, measures, almost-everywhere properties, scalar and Banach-valued integration, product measures and convergence of integrals.

Knowledge: Sigma-algebras specify measurability, while null sets define almost-everywhere equivalence. Monotone convergence applies to increasing nonnegative measurable functions, and dominated convergence relies on an integrable common bound together with the appropriate convergence and measurability. On sigma-finite product spaces, Tonelli treats nonnegative integrands and Fubini treats integrable functions. Bochner integrability of a Banach-valued function requires strong measurability and integrability of its norm. Absolute continuity and Radon-Nikodym derivatives describe changes of measure under the relevant measure hypotheses.

Distinctions: An extended nonnegative integral differs from a finite scalar or vector integral. Borel measurability and measurability after completion need not coincide. Almost-everywhere equality preserves integrals but does not identify pointwise values, and pointwise convergence alone supplies no expectation convergence.

Connections: Probability interprets integrals as expectations, real analysis studies differentiation and integration, and functional analysis organizes Lp spaces. Geometric measure theory and harmonic analysis add geometric and frequency structure.

Example: On [0,1], let λ be Lebesgue measure and ν=2λ+δ₀. Both measures are finite, and ν splits into the absolutely continuous part 2λ with density 2 and the singular part δ₀ supported on the λ-null singleton {0}. No λ-integrable density can represent all of ν, because its integral over {0} would be zero whereas ν({0})=1. For a continuous test function φ, integration gives ∫φ dν=2∫₀¹φ(x)dx+φ(0). In particular, ν([0,1])=3 while integrating the density 2 alone yields 2. Altering the density at the single point zero cannot recover the missing atom, since densities are defined only almost everywhere.

## geometric-measure-theory

**几何测度论 / Geometric Measure Theory Expert** (D046)

Expertise: Specialist in Hausdorff measure and dimension, rectifiable sets, density, functions of bounded variation, finite-perimeter sets and measure-theoretic geometric surfaces.

Knowledge: Hausdorff measures record size through covers at small scales, with normalization affecting their relation to Euclidean volume. Countable rectifiability describes sets covered, up to a null set, by countably many Lipschitz images of the relevant dimension. Area and coarea formulas for Lipschitz maps include Jacobians and multiplicities. A set of locally finite perimeter has an indicator with locally bounded variation; in Euclidean space its perimeter measure is concentrated on a countably rectifiable reduced boundary.

Distinctions: Topological boundary and reduced boundary retain different information. Image measure differs from an integral that counts multiple preimages. Tangent or density statements may hold only almost everywhere, and local finite measure does not imply global finite mass or smoothness.

Connections: Measure theory supplies integration and null sets, differential geometry describes smooth special cases, and the calculus of variations studies perimeter minimization. Sobolev and BV theory connect weak derivatives to geometric structure, while currents encode oriented integration.

Example: Consider the Lipschitz map f:[−1,1]→R², f(t)=(t²,0). Its speed is |f′(t)|=2|t|, so the parameterized length integral is ∫_{−1}¹2|t|dt=2. Its image is the unit segment [0,1]×{0}, whose normalized one-dimensional Hausdorff measure is one. Every interior image point (s,0), 0<s<1, has the two preimages ±√s; the endpoint multiplicities do not affect the integral. The area formula therefore counts the segment with multiplicity two and recovers length two. Forgetting the parametrization leaves only image length one. This distinguishes an image set from its multiplicity-bearing measure, even for a smooth map with a simple one-dimensional image.

## operator-theory

**算子理论 / Operator Theory Expert** (D047)

Expertise: Specialist in bounded and unbounded linear operators, adjoints, resolvents, spectra, compactness and spectral structure on Banach and Hilbert spaces.

Knowledge: A bounded operator on a nonzero complex Banach space has nonempty compact spectrum, and its resolvent varies analytically off the spectrum. Compact operators have restricted nonzero spectral behavior; compact self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces have an orthogonal spectral decomposition with finite-dimensional nonzero eigenspaces. Densely defined operators admit adjoints under the appropriate Hilbert-space setup. For unbounded operators, self-adjointness includes equality of domains with the adjoint, and spectral representations involve projection-valued measures rather than only finite matrices.

Distinctions: Symmetry is weaker than self-adjointness for unbounded operators. Domain equality is part of operator equality and affects sums, products and commutators. Operator-norm, strong and weak operator convergence differ. Real and complex spectra need not have the same properties, and compactness alone does not imply diagonalizability.

Connections: Functional analysis supplies the ambient spaces and duality, quantum mechanics uses self-adjoint observables, and differential equations produce unbounded differential operators. Numerical linear algebra approximates spectral data with additional convergence and conditioning questions.

Example: On complex ℓ²({1,2,…}), define (Ax)ₙ=nxₙ with domain D(A)={x:Σn²|xₙ|²<∞}. Testing the adjoint identity on unit vectors forces (A*y)ₙ=nyₙ; this belongs to ℓ² exactly for y∈D(A), so A is self-adjoint. The vector xₙ=1/n belongs to ℓ² but lies outside D(A), since Ax would be the constant sequence one. Spectral projections select coordinates whose indices lie in a chosen Borel set. In particular, the cutoffs Pₘ onto indices 1,…,m converge strongly to the identity by square-summable tails, while ‖I−Pₘ‖=1 for every m. The domain restriction and convergence topology each carry information absent from the diagonal formula alone.

## approximation-theory

**逼近论 / Approximation Theory Expert** (D048)

Expertise: Specialist in polynomial, trigonometric, spline and rational approximation, density, best approximation and quantitative dependence of approximation error on regularity.

Knowledge: Weierstrass approximation concerns continuous functions on compact real intervals. Stone-Weierstrass extends density to suitable function algebras on compact Hausdorff spaces; real algebras need constants and point separation, while complex versions also require conjugation closure. Jackson-type theorems relate approximation rates to moduli of smoothness under specified norms and domains. Finite-dimensional subspaces of normed spaces admit best approximants, though uniqueness need not hold. Closed linear subspaces of Hilbert spaces instead have unique orthogonal best approximants.

Distinctions: Density does not itself provide a rate, a stable interpolation scheme or uniqueness of a nearest approximant. Uniform and integral errors describe different guarantees. Rational approximation depends on pole locations, and interpolation node geometry affects stability even for smooth target functions.

Connections: Real and harmonic analysis describe function regularity and spectral expansions. Convex geometry explains extremality and uniqueness, while numerical analysis studies stable representations, rounding effects and computable error control.

Example: Approximate f(x)=|x| on [−1,1] by real polynomials of degree at most one in the uniform norm. The constant p*(x)=1/2 has error f−p* equal to 1/2 at −1, −1/2 at 0 and 1/2 at 1, with absolute error nowhere exceeding 1/2. These three alternating extreme points meet the degree-one equioscillation condition, so p* is the unique best approximant. In contrast, the line interpolating f at both endpoints is the constant one and has error one at zero. Exact agreement at the selected nodes therefore gives twice the optimal worst-case error. The relevant objective is a uniform bound over the entire interval, rather than the interpolation constraints alone.

## distributions-sobolev

**广义函数与Sobolev空间 / Distributions and Sobolev Spaces Expert** (D049)

Expertise: Specialist in distributions, weak derivatives, Sobolev and related function spaces, traces, embeddings and the interpretation of differential identities below classical smoothness.

Knowledge: Distributions are continuous linear functionals on smooth compactly supported test functions, and their derivatives are defined by duality with a sign. Sobolev spaces W^{k,p} consist of Lp functions whose weak derivatives through order k also belong to Lp. Embedding exponents depend on dimension, derivative order and integrability; critical and subcritical regimes differ. Trace theorems depend on boundary regularity, and Rellich-type compactness also requires suitable domain and exponent hypotheses. Representatives are generally identified only almost everywhere.

Distinctions: A classical derivative away from exceptional points need not be the distributional derivative. Weak differentiability, pointwise differentiability and continuity are distinct properties. Boundary values are not automatically point evaluations, and a distribution need not be represented by a locally integrable function.

Connections: Measure theory supplies equivalence classes and integration, functional analysis supplies duality, and partial differential equations use weak solution spaces. Geometric measure theory connects BV derivatives and singular measures to surfaces.

Example: On (−1,1), the step function h=1_{x>0} has classical derivative zero away from zero, yet its distributional derivative is δ₀: for every smooth compactly supported φ, −∫₀¹φ′(x)dx=φ(0). The point mass is not represented by a locally integrable function, so h is not in W^{1,p} for any 1≤p≤∞. By contrast, |x| has weak derivative sign(x) in L∞ and belongs to W^{1,∞} on this interval, although it is not classically differentiable at zero. Its second distributional derivative is 2δ₀, excluding W^{2,p} for the same p range. Singular derivatives distinguish jumps from corners and remain visible even when exceptional points have Lebesgue measure zero.

## potential-theory

**位势论 / Potential Theory Expert** (D050)

Expertise: Specialist in harmonic and subharmonic functions, Green functions, Newtonian or logarithmic potentials, capacities, energy and boundary regularity for elliptic phenomena.

Knowledge: Classical harmonic functions satisfy the Laplace equation and local mean-value properties. On Euclidean domains, Weyl's lemma relates a locally integrable distributional harmonic function to a smooth harmonic representative. Maximum principles constrain harmonic functions with suitable domain and boundary behavior. Green functions incorporate both a fundamental singularity and a domain-dependent boundary condition. Capacity measures the potential-theoretic significance of small sets, and Wiener-type criteria connect capacity to regularity of boundary points for the classical Dirichlet problem.

Distinctions: Measure-zero sets need not have zero capacity. Weak harmonicity, a classical representative and attainment of boundary data express different information. Uniqueness on unbounded domains can require growth or decay assumptions; the Laplacian sign and kernel normalization affect potential formulas.

Connections: Elliptic partial differential equations study the associated boundary problems, probability connects harmonic measures with Brownian motion, and complex analysis supplies planar harmonic functions. Functional analysis interprets energy spaces and weak formulations.

Example: In R³ take the exterior domain Ω={x:|x|>1} and u(x)=1−1/|x|. Writing r=|x| gives u′(r)=r⁻² and u″(r)=−2r⁻³, hence Δu=u″+(2/r)u′=0 throughout Ω. The function is smooth, bounded and continuous up to the unit sphere, where its boundary value is zero. Nevertheless u is positive inside and tends to one at infinity; the zero function has the same finite-boundary data. Thus bounded harmonic solutions of this exterior Dirichlet problem are not unique without a condition at infinity. The usual bounded-domain maximum principle cannot replace its domain hypothesis with boundedness of the solution alone.
